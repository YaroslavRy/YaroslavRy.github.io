Neuron Sim

Specimen 04 WebGL · gl.POINTS Leaky integrate-and-fire · sparse connectivity

Every point is a neuron: it leaks charge toward rest, occasionally fires on its own, and — once it crosses threshold — kicks a set of other neurons. In realistic mode those connections are distance-based: nearby neurons are more likely to be linked, connection strength decays with distance, and the signal takes longer to arrive the farther it travels — you can watch this directly, each firing neuron sends a small bright pulse traveling along a faint line to every neuron it's about to affect, arriving exactly when the delay says it will. Random mode is a control condition: same neuron count, but every connection target is picked uniformly at random anywhere in the population, delivered instantly, no visible travel. Switch between them below and watch the raster and phase portrait change shape.

The phase portrait plots population spiking activity against its rate of change, one dot per frame, with old dots fading — this is what makes it possible to see the dynamics rather than just the raw spikes. Distance-based connectivity tends to produce tighter, more structured loops (local clusters syncing up); fully random connectivity tends to look noisier and less organized, since there's no spatial structure for activity to travel coherently through. Scales to roughly 10,000+ neurons depending on your device's GPU — rendering is a single WebGL draw call per frame regardless of count, so the ceiling is mostly how much your hardware can push.

How it actually works

Each neuron is a leaky integrate-and-fire unit. Every step its membrane potential relaxes toward rest, occasionally gets nudged up by spontaneous noise, and if it crosses threshold it fires, resets, and sits in a brief refractory period:

$$ V_i(t{+}1) = V_i(t) + \alpha \left( V_{rest} - V_i(t) \right) + I_i(t) $$

where $V_i$ is neuron $i$'s potential, $\alpha$ is the leak rate, and $I_i(t)$ is any incoming signal arriving this step. A spike fires when $V_i \geq V_{th}$, after which $V_i \to V_{rest}$ and the neuron ignores input for $T_{ref}$ steps.

In realistic mode, both the odds of a connection existing and its strength fall off with distance $d_{ij}$ between two neurons — closer neurons are more likely to be wired together, and more strongly:

$$ P(i \to j) = e^{-d_{ij}/\lambda} \qquad\qquad w_{ij} = w_0 \cdot \operatorname{sign}(i) \cdot e^{-d_{ij}/\lambda} $$

Signals also don't arrive instantly — they travel at an effective fixed speed, so delay scales with distance too:

$$ \Delta t_{ij} = \left\lceil \dfrac{d_{ij}}{r_{max}} \cdot D_{max} \right\rceil $$

$\lambda$ is the decay length, $r_{max}$ the local search radius, and $D_{max}$ the maximum delay in steps. Pure locality like this, though, only lets activity ripple outward through nearest neighbors — it can never sync distant parts of the network, which isn't how real cortex works. So ~18% of each neuron's connections deliberately skip the radius and link to a random distant neuron instead — a sparse long-range layer on top of the dense local one, the same small-world mix (dense local wiring + sparse long-distance projections) real cortical connectivity has. Those edges use a wider decay length and a longer delay cap, which is why you'll see the occasional pulse travel slowly clear across the canvas instead of only ever hopping to its immediate neighbors.
